Last Call, by Bike

Montréal’s bike-share data shows a city that commutes like clockwork by day. After dark it tells a different story, down to the minute the bars close.

By Zachary D. Lewin · November 2026 · Analysis of 23.7 million BIXI trips, January 2025 to August 2026

Monday to Friday, every morning, I ride a BIXI down to McGill’s campus and play my favourite game of hide-and-seek: me against an open dock. I aim for the University / Milton stop; if it’s full, McTavish / Sherbrooke is a sound second choice. By a quarter to nine both are usually packed, and I circle the block while the clock runs down on my first lecture. Then, when class lets out in the late afternoon, the same stations that had no room for my bike now have no bike for me.

It feels like bad luck. It isn’t. It’s a tide, a natural oscillation of to-ing and fro-ing around the city. I first started tracking these tides to make my commute easier, but the city quickly began to look different, especially after dark.

Between 5 a.m. and noon on a school day, those stations take in about 440 more bikes than they send out. At the 8 o’clock peak, around 200 bikes arrive and only 34 leave. By late afternoon the flow reverses, and by 5 p.m. the campus is draining twice as fast as it fills.

No other campus pulls like it. Around UQAM, the stations take in about 250 more bikes than they send out on a school morning. Around Concordia’s downtown campus, barely 40. Université de Montréal, on the far side of the mountain, has only five stations within the same distance.

But the pull isn’t only academic. Every weekday morning, the whole city leans downtown.

This is every BIXI station that saw at least 2,000 trips since January 2025: more than 1,100 docks that trace the city’s street grid from Verdun to Villeray.

Bigger circles mean more trips start there. Brighter circles are stations used far more at this hour than the rest of the week; dim ones are quiet at night.

The Main

The night has a spine: St-Laurent Boulevard, known locally as the Main. Eleven of the city’s 25 busiest late-night stations sit on it or one block off, on Clark or St-Dominique.

Milton / Clark, steps from the nightclub Palazo, sees 3.7 times its normal share of trips at that hour.

The Village

A second bright cluster runs along Ste-Catherine Street East through the Village. Métro Beaudry and St-Timothée / Ste-Catherine see more than four times their usual share, the highest of any station with at least 1,000 late-night trips.

Going home

And then everyone goes home. Lines show the most common late-night routes. Orange stations send out more riders than they receive; blue ones are where riders end up.

Between 1 and 4 a.m. on weekends, the Plateau and downtown start about 40,000 more trips than they receive, roughly one in six of the late-night trips that begin there. The night empties outward, to Rosemont, Villeray, Hochelaga and the Sud-Ouest.

There are also those first few fresh weeks of the school year in Montréal, before the wind and the snow and the chill, when you can still go out in a T-shirt and jeans. Prince-Arthur is car-free, the terraces spill onto the sidewalks like the drunks who fill them, and the whole strip feels like one long party. In Québec, a night like that is called virer une brosse, literally “turning a brush.” Then 3 a.m. comes, the bars throw everyone out, and you watch the great migration: thousands of university students working out how to get home, some throwing up in side streets, others making out in alleyways.

That exact moment exists within the data.

On weekday nights, ridership fades steadily toward dawn. On weekends it fades as well, until 3 a.m., when it rises again for the first time since the evening. It’s a small bump, a few thousand trips over 20 months, but it shows up exclusively on weekend nights. Last call, in the shape of a curve.

The busiest nights of all line up with the city’s calendar.

The Saturday of Osheaga, the music festival, tops the list. The Friday of the 2025 Grand Prix weekend is third. Halloween 2025, a Friday at the end of October, made the top 10 despite the cold.

What anonymous rides reveal

None of these trips carries a name. BIXI publishes only where each ride started, where it ended and when. That is enough to show where and when Montréal is working, where it drinks and when it goes home, at the level of a single street corner.

In 2018, the fitness app Strava published a global heatmap of its users’ activity, also aggregated and anonymous. Analysts soon noticed that it traced the outlines of military bases, and the routines of the people inside them. A dataset with no names can still describe a pattern of life.

BIXI’s data carries no rider IDs, so it describes the city, not any individual citizen within it. It is also a reminder of how thin the line is between a city’s pattern and a person’s. What happens on the Main at 3 a.m. may stay there. The data, it turns out, remembers.

About the data

Trip records come from BIXI Montréal’s open data, January 2025 to August 2026: 23,753,414 trips with start and end stations and timestamps, converted to Montréal local time. “Late night” means trips starting between 1:00 and 3:59 a.m. on Saturday and Sunday mornings, that is, Friday and Saturday nights. A station’s late-night share compares its share of those trips with its share of all trips; stations with fewer than 2,000 trips are excluded. Campus figures use stations within about 450 metres of each campus (22 for McGill, 34 for UQAM, 26 for Concordia, 5 for Université de Montréal), on weekdays in April and September to November. Station positions are averaged across the period. BIXI rebalances bikes by truck, and those moves are not in the public data, so the flows here describe riders, not dock levels. Borough boundaries: Ville de Montréal open data. Analysis in Python and DuckDB; graphics in D3.

Other sources: Québec’s 3 a.m. last call (Global News, Bloomberg); Strava heatmap and military bases (The Guardian, The Washington Post, 2018); event dates from Formula 1 and Osheaga.
