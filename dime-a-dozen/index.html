Dime a Dozen

From Chapter One

London in the autumn of 1945 smelled of wet ash, river rot, and a kind of stale, leftover patriotism that nobody had yet found the heart to throw away. The bombsites were still steaming in places where the rain found something to react with, and the boys back home from Burma walked with a stiffness in the hips that no amount of time spent at pubs along the East end could quite shake out. It was a city that was surviving itself, and one that would keep on doing so, so long as it was given the chance.

On a corner of Cheapside, a baker in a flat cap was bellowing his lungs out over a crate of pallid loaves. “Two shillings for a loaf! Two shillings, missus, and you’ll not see better this side of Christmas! Two shillings for a loaf!” His voice carried the weight of years shouting prices at the same patch of pavement since before the Blitz, but his stature seemed as if he could keep on doing so through another war.

Across the road, in the dim light of an alley between a tobacconist’s and a boarded-up milliner, a different kind of negotiation was occurring. A thin man in a dusty grey suit was being driven repeatedly into a brick wall by a larger fellow, who worked with the unhurried economy of a butcher hack-sawing his meats. There was but a whimper. Only the deep, periodic percussion of a body meeting masonry, and then, the soft crescendo of coins falling out of a ripped pocket onto the ground. Two shillings and a penny, I could tell by the song they sung. The larger fellow bent down, grabbed what was there and disregarded the mess left behind.

The man in the flat cap crossed the street and handed the thin man a loaf. “I know you’re good for it, you can give me four shillings next time.” The thin man looked up and smiled.

❦

That was all I remembered from the glimpse I got of the outside. I originally come from The Royal Mint, Tower Hill, two roads east of the alley way, behind a wall of dressed Portland stone and a pair of iron gates that had not been opened in ceremony for the past six years. It was a grey, pillared, soot-stained lump that looked less like the source of the British Empire’s coins than it did a court house reserved for whores and sex-fiends. That was my home, not for long though, they make sure to get you out of there and onto the streets quickly.

Bag 18100. Fifth row from the door, second tier from the floor, in a canvas sack stencilled ROYAL MINT — SHILLINGS — 5000, lashed at the neck with hemp twine and a lead seal the size of a thumbnail. There are four thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine of my fellow brothers and sisters side by side.

How I came to be here is a story of old. Coins are as old as people themselves. As soon as there were people there were objects they used to signify value. I was not always a shilling. Before that, I was a great many other things, a sea-shell, grain, livestock, jewellery, weaponry. But for the present, in this autumn of 1945, I am a shilling of the realm, the property of His Majesty’s Government, fifty per cent silver, forty per cent copper, five per cent nickel and five per cent zinc, and I am about to be put into the wild hands of the British public, under the power of their spending. But for each coin spent is a coin that had to be made.

The chapter goes on: how a shilling is made, from Broken Hill silver to the coining press on Tower Hill, and what waits in the vault.
