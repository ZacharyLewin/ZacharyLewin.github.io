Dime a Dozen

Chapter One

London in the autumn of 1945 smelled of wet ash, river rot, and a kind of stale, leftover patriotism that nobody had yet found the heart to throw away. The bombsites were still steaming in places where the rain found something to react with, and the boys back home from Burma walked with a stiffness in the hips that no amount of time spent at pubs along the East end could quite shake out. It was a city that was surviving itself, and one that would keep on doing so, so long as it was given the chance.

On a corner of Cheapside, a baker in a flat cap was bellowing his lungs out over a crate of pallid loaves. “Two shillings for a loaf! Two shillings, missus, and you’ll not see better this side of Christmas! Two shillings for a loaf!” His voice carried the weight of years shouting prices at the same patch of pavement since before the Blitz, but his stature seemed as if he could keep on doing so through another war.

Across the road, in the dim light of an alley between a tobacconist’s and a boarded-up milliner, a different kind of negotiation was occurring. A thin man in a dusty grey suit was being driven repeatedly into a brick wall by a larger fellow, who worked with the unhurried economy of a butcher hack-sawing his meats. There was but a whimper. Only the deep, periodic percussion of a body meeting masonry, and then, the soft crescendo of coins falling out of a ripped pocket onto the ground. Two shillings and a penny, I could tell by the song they sung. The larger fellow bent down, grabbed what was there and disregarded the mess left behind.

The man in the flat cap crossed the street and handed the thin man a loaf. “I know you’re good for it, you can give me four shillings next time.” The thin man looked up and smiled.

That was all I remembered from the glimpse I got of the outside. I originally come from The Royal Mint, Tower Hill, two roads east of the alley way, behind a wall of dressed Portland stone and a pair of iron gates that had not been opened in ceremony for the past six years. It was a grey, pillared, soot-stained lump that looked less like the source of the British Empire’s coins than it did a court house reserved for whores and sex-fiends. That was my home, not for long though, they make sure to get you out of there and onto the streets quickly.

Bag 18100. Fifth row from the door, second tier from the floor, in a canvas sack stencilled ROYAL MINT — SHILLINGS — 5000, lashed at the neck with hemp twine and a lead seal the size of a thumbnail. There are four thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine of my fellow brothers and sisters side by side.

How I came to be here is a story of old. Coins are as old as people themselves. As soon as there were people there were objects they used to signify value. I was not always a shilling. Before that, I was a great many other things, a sea-shell, grain, livestock, jewellery, weaponry. But for the present, in this autumn of 1945, I am a shilling of the realm, the property of His Majesty’s Government, fifty per cent silver, forty per cent copper, five per cent nickel and five per cent zinc, and I am about to be put into the wild hands of the British public, under the power of their spending. But for each coin spent is a coin that had to be made.

❦

The making of a coin is, in the abstract, a simple business. A government decides it requires money. It enters into contracts with mines and refiners; it buys metal; it melts the metal; it stamps a likeness of the sovereign (King George VI) upon said metal; and said metal becomes, by a kind of practised civic alchemy, currency, valuable, like livestock during times of famine, or weaponry during times of war.

Now, with regards to my actual constitution: Before the Great War the shilling had been a more dignified object than the one I am: sterling silver, with the cool, slightly bluish lustre of a coin that knows what it is. By 1920 this dignity had been placed out of the budget. So the alloy was cut with copper, nickel and a pinch of zinc, and the shillings came out of the press with a faintly warmer cast. We were not as handsome as our grandfathers. We were, however, much more numerous, and in 1945 proper dignity was not above what’s necessary.

My silver came from Broken Hill, in New South Wales, a place I have never seen and will never see, a vast red wound in the Australian outback where the lodes ran so rich the earliest miners are said to have walked over the stuff for a year before they understood what they were treading on. The silver was refined, drawn into ingots, and shipped from Port Pirie across the Indian Ocean in the hold of a tramp steamer whose name no one bothered to write down. My copper and my nickel travelled a different road. They were dug at Sudbury, in Ontario, Canada. Smelted in furnaces that had been running flat out since 1939, and brought across the Atlantic in convoy.

The ingots arrived at the London docks under guard and were transported up to Tower Hill in a lorry whose plates had been smeared with mud, on the theory that a clearly marked van full of metals might cause the larger fellows a bit of temptation. At the Mint they were received, weighed, signed for in a ledger by a man with ink on his cuff, and conveyed to the rolling mills.

There the metal was passed back and forth between great hardened cylinders until it had been beaten down into long pale strips, ribbons of alloy no thicker than a playing card. From the strips, machines punched out blanks; flat, featureless discs called planchets, which is what I once was. The planchets were heated until they glowed faintly and then quenched, in order to soften them up; they were bathed in a weak acid to strip the oxidation; they were fed into an upsetting mill which raised a small, neat lip around their edges, so that they would strike cleanly and stack without sliding. That is the technical phrase, upset, I always thought it should have been setup, but I wasn’t consulted at the time. And then the dies, another stupid naming decision. It’s the thing that creates the impression that gets struck onto the coin, call it the design, or the impression, dies makes no sense, but that’s coinage, it’s a bizarre industry that never gets any customer feedback.

Somewhere in the engraving room of the Mint, in a quiet office that smelled of oil and cigarette smoke, a master engraver named Thomas Humphrey Paget had years before cut the king’s portrait in steel, truly beautiful work, left-facing, bare-headed, the jaw set in that careful, slightly weary way. From Paget’s master hub a working die had been struck, and from that working die another, and so the king’s face had been multiplied, in negative, down through a small dynasty of steel until at last there was a die hard enough and sharp enough to be bolted into the press that was waiting for me.

I do not remember most of what came before the press. I remember it the way one remembers a bad fever. Blasts of heat, horrible pressure, and the conviction that something was being done to me that I had not consented to and could not have refused. I was an ingot, and then I was a strip, and then I was a disc, and through all of it I was, in some sense I was just asleep. An infant in the womb of the Royal Mint’s maternity ward, drowsing through the various indignities of gestation, aware of warmth and motion and not much else.

Then I was fed into the coining press. Et voilà.

The press came down with a force strong enough to bring me to being. Both of my faces were impressed at once, the obverse and the reverse, and a collar around my circumference milled my edge into the small parallel grooves that mark me as a shilling and not a sixpence or a florin. The whole event took something less than a quarter of a second. But at the end, there I was.

It’s a bizarre moment when we become ourselves. I remember a great scream, then a silent scream and a sharp noise, in the aftermath I knew, with a clarity I have not since reattained, that I am, and had been. I was a shilling. I was His Majesty King George VI, in profile, on one side; I was the lion of England, crowned, on the other; I was twelve pence sterling, one twentieth of a pound, legal tender throughout the United Kingdom and her dominions for the discharge of any debt up to forty shillings. I was struck on a Tuesday, in the third week of October, in a room that smelled of piss, oil and stifling metal and the sultry sweat of three men in short-sleeved shirts who had not looked at me long enough to register that I was now alive.

Why should they have? They had struck two hundred thousand of us that morning already. I shouldn’t have been any more special than any of my cousins or siblings.

❦

From the press we were swept, still tepid, into hoppers and back out the hoppers into counting machines, which clattered through us with the thoughtless speed of a hellcat on an open highway. Five thousand of us were tipped into a canvas bag; sixty-two pounds of newborn coins, the neck of the bag was twisted, sealed with a lead button stamped: ‘R M’, and tossed onto a pallet with a hundred others.

That afternoon the pallet was loaded into the back of an armoured Crossley with a corporal of the Mint Guard riding in the cab and a constable of the City of London Police following behind on motorcycle. We were driven west along Cannon Street, past the wreckage of St. Mildred’s and the reconstructions at Mansion House, and turned up Threadneedle Street to the Bank of England, the Old Bitch herself, with her great grey windowless façade, the matriarch of the nation’s wealth, into whose vaults we were carried by men who did not speak above a murmur and who treated each bag as if it might detonate, blowing up the old Bitch.

There, in a basement somewhere beneath the City, we were added into the books, my life officially official. We ceased, in that instant, to be five thousand newly-struck shillings and became a credit to the Treasury, a small contribution to the total reserves. It is a strange thing to be entered into a ledger. One feels, briefly, important and then one is shelved and reminded of one’s insignificance, like how an astronaut must feel in space; special that they are the one chosen to go explore the vast expanse, but at the sight of the Earth in totality, how can one escape that hopeless feeling of minuscule being, of our ownmost insignificance.

And thus, I have been shelved. And now I lie. It’s cold in the vault, the darkness encompasses all that’s around me, and the only song sung is the occasional shifting of a sister or brother or cousin of mine against my side. The dry brush of canvas on canvas as the bag settles by half an inch into its place.

❦

But there is news. There is always news in the vault, even when one cannot see where it comes from. Money, as I have come to understand, is the great whisperer, like Hermes, the Greek messenger God, it reveals the truth behind our desires, it tells us a story each time it exchanges hands. Today’s top story is this: the Barclays branch in Holborn has put in a request to the Bank of England for five hundred shillings, against payrolls due at the end of the week.

Five hundred shillings. One tenth of my bag.

I have lain here, in this dark, for eleven days. I have listened to the porters lift bags off the shelf and carry them out, and I have known, every time, that it was not me. I have heard the great door of the vault open and shut, open and shut, and each time it shut on the wrong side of my hopes. But five hundred shillings, drawn for a Barclays payroll in Holborn; this must be my time.

I want, more than I have ever wanted anything in my brief and almost exclusively horizontal existence, to be among those five hundred. I want to feel a hand close around me. I want to ride in a satchel through the streets of a city. I want to be counted out across a counter into the palm of a merchant, and I want, in the end, to be spent. Oh, what a dream for me to be passed on from hand to hand through the great commerce of London.

Maybe one day I’ll even end up in the hands of the kind baker in the flat cap. I have heard him in my sleep. “Two shillings for a loaf, missus. Two shillings for a loaf.” And then I felt it, it was my bag being lifted, it was my bag being opened. The world was finally unveiling itself for me and I cannot wait to see what life the market will bring me.
